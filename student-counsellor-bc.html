Seeing a student counsellor in BC: what to expect, what it costs, and whether it's right for you.

Hilary McKee Starting Therapy 8 min read Updated November 3, 2026

The short answer

A student counsellor (also called an intern counsellor) is a graduate student in a master's counselling program who sees clients under the supervision of an experienced, registered professional. Sessions usually cost much less than private therapy. In Vancouver, intern sessions are often $60 to $65, and some clinics offer a sliding scale on top of that. Students meet with a supervisor every week, follow the same confidentiality standards as other counsellors, and are trained in current approaches. For many people, it's a good way to start therapy without the full private-practice price.

If you've been looking for affordable therapy in BC, you've probably come across the words "intern" or "student counsellor" and wondered what they mean. Is it real therapy? Will they know what they're doing? Who else hears what you say?

I'm a counselling psychology student myself, and from January 2027 I'll be seeing clients as a student counsellor at East Vancouver Counselling. So I want to answer these questions as plainly as I can, including the parts that might give you pause.

What is a student counsellor?

A student counsellor is someone in the final stage of a master's degree in counselling or counselling psychology. Before they can graduate, they complete a practicum: a placement at a clinic or agency where they work with real clients while being supervised.

By the time students reach practicum, they've usually completed most of their coursework in counselling theory, ethics, assessment and specific approaches. Practicum is where they put that training into practice, with support.

You might see different names for the same thing: student counsellor, intern counsellor, counselling intern, practicum student or student therapist.

How supervision works

Supervision is the main thing that sets student counsellors apart, and it's a big part of why seeing one can be a good choice.

Every student counsellor works under a supervisor, who is an experienced professional such as a Registered Clinical Counsellor (RCC) or registered psychologist. The student meets with them regularly to talk through their work, get feedback and make sure clients are getting good care.

In my program, for example, students need at least one hour of individual supervision every week during practicum. Supervisors also directly observe some sessions, either live or through recordings, and students complete 200 hours of direct work with clients before they finish.

In practice, this means your counsellor isn't working alone. Someone experienced is keeping an eye on the work and helping your counsellor think about how best to support you.

What does it cost?

This is where student counsellors make the biggest difference. Private counselling in Vancouver often costs well over $100 a session. Student counsellors usually charge much less.

One thing to know: many extended health plans only cover sessions with certain registered professionals, such as RCCs or psychologists. Sessions with a student counsellor often aren't covered, so ask your plan before you book if you're hoping to claim. Even without coverage, intern rates are often lower than what you'd pay out of pocket after benefits for private therapy.

Is a student counsellor good enough?

This is the question most people are really asking, so here's my honest answer.

Research consistently finds that the relationship between you and your counsellor is one of the strongest predictors of whether therapy helps. A large review of 295 studies and more than 30,000 clients found a clear link between the strength of that relationship (sometimes called the therapeutic alliance) and how much people improved.

Experience matters too, but perhaps less than you'd expect. One study followed 170 therapists and more than 6,500 clients for up to 18 years and found that, on average, therapists' results didn't improve just with more years of practice. That doesn't mean students are better than experienced therapists. It means years in practice alone don't decide whether therapy will work for you.

There are also some real advantages to working with a student:

  • Close supervision. Most experienced therapists in private practice don't have someone reviewing their work every week. Students do.
  • Current training. Students are learning the most up-to-date research and approaches right now.
  • Time and care. Students usually see fewer clients and often spend more time preparing for and reflecting on each session.
  • Lower cost. Paying less can make it possible to come regularly, which matters for how much therapy helps.

Seeing a student isn't right for everyone. If you're in crisis, need specialized care for something complex, or want a specific type of treatment that requires advanced certification, an experienced registered professional may be a better fit. A good student counsellor will tell you if they think you need something they can't offer, and help you find it.

What about confidentiality?

What you share with a student counsellor is confidential, with the same limits that apply to any counsellor. These limits include situations where someone's safety is at serious risk, or where the law requires a disclosure.

The main difference is that your counsellor will talk about their work with their supervisor. Supervisors are bound by confidentiality too. In BC, professional standards say counsellors should explain how confidentiality works, including for students and interns, at the start of counselling, and should generally get your consent before sharing information for supervision.

If your counsellor would like to record a session for supervision, they should ask you first and explain how the recording will be stored and when it will be deleted. It's always okay to ask questions about this, or to say you're not comfortable with it.

What the first session is like

A first session with a student counsellor is very similar to one with any other counsellor. You'll usually:

  • go over consent, confidentiality and how supervision works
  • talk about what's bringing you in and what you're hoping for
  • share a bit of your background, at whatever pace feels okay
  • ask any questions you have about how they work

You don't need to know exactly what to say or have everything figured out. Part of the first session is simply noticing how it feels to talk with this person.

Many clinics, including East Vancouver Counselling, offer a free consultation first. It's a good chance to ask questions before you commit.

Questions to ask a student counsellor

You're always allowed to ask about someone's training. A few questions that can help:

  • What program are you in, and how far along are you?
  • Who is your supervisor, and what is their background?
  • How long will you be at this clinic? What happens when your placement ends?
  • Have you had training in the kind of support I'm looking for?
  • Will any of our sessions be recorded, and how is that handled?

The question about placement length is worth asking. Practicum placements have an end date, so it helps to know ahead of time whether you could keep seeing the same counsellor, move to another counsellor at the clinic, or be referred somewhere else.

How to find a student counsellor in BC

  • Training clinics: Places like the UBC Psychology Clinic and the Adler Centre in Vancouver offer sessions with supervised students.
  • Private clinics with interns: Many group practices have intern counsellors. Search for "intern," "student counsellor" or "low-cost" on clinic websites, or simply call and ask.
  • East Vancouver Counselling: Their intern counsellors see adults, couples, children and youth, in person on Commercial Drive and online across BC.

For more options, including free programs, see my guide to free and low-cost counselling in BC. If you're comparing titles, my post on counsellors, psychotherapists, psychologists and RCCs in BC explains what each one means.

Frequently asked questions

What is a student counsellor?

A student counsellor is a graduate student in a master's counselling program who sees clients during their practicum, under the regular supervision of an experienced, registered professional.

How much does a student counsellor cost in Vancouver?

Intern sessions in Vancouver are often listed at $60 to $65, and some clinics offer a sliding scale. Training clinics like the UBC Psychology Clinic have listed sliding-scale fees from about $10 to $50. Check current rates with each clinic.

Are student counsellors covered by extended health benefits?

Often not. Many plans only cover sessions with specific registered professionals, such as Registered Clinical Counsellors or psychologists. Check with your plan before booking if you're hoping to claim.

Is a student counsellor as good as a regular therapist?

Research suggests the relationship between you and your counsellor is one of the strongest predictors of whether therapy helps, and that years of experience alone don't guarantee better results. Student counsellors are closely supervised, and many people have a very good experience with them.

Is therapy with a student counsellor confidential?

Yes, with the same limits that apply to any counsellor. Your counsellor will discuss their work with their supervisor, who is also bound by confidentiality, and should explain how this works at the start.

What happens when a student counsellor's placement ends?

Practicum placements have an end date. Ask at the start whether you'll be able to keep working with the same counsellor, move to another counsellor at the clinic, or be referred elsewhere.

The bottom line

Seeing a student counsellor isn't a lesser version of therapy. It's supervised, lower-cost care from someone who is putting a lot of care into learning to do this well. If cost has been holding you back, it's worth considering.
