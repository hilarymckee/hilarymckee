Gottman Method couples therapy: what it is and what to expect.

Hilary McKee Couples & Relationships 10 min read Updated November 17, 2026

The short answer

The Gottman Method is a structured approach to couples therapy based on more than 40 years of research on what happy, stable couples do differently. It usually starts with an assessment over about three sessions: one together, one individual session each, and a feedback session where you set goals. After that, sessions focus on practising skills together, like handling conflict more gently, calming down when things get heated, and rebuilding friendship and connection. The goal isn't for you to stop disagreeing. It's for your next hard conversation to go better than your last one.

If you've started looking for couples counselling in Vancouver, you've probably seen "Gottman" listed on a lot of therapist profiles. It's one of the best-known approaches to couples therapy, but most descriptions stop at "research-based" without explaining what actually happens in the room.

I recently completed Gottman Method Level 1 training, and I'll be seeing couples at East Vancouver Counselling from January 2027. Here's a plain-language guide to how it works, so you can decide whether it feels like a fit.

What is the Gottman Method?

The Gottman Method was developed by Drs. John and Julie Gottman. It grew out of decades of research in which John Gottman and his colleagues watched couples talk, argue and spend time together, then followed them for years to see which relationships lasted and which didn't.

The research found that the couples who did well weren't the ones who never fought. They fought differently. They brought up problems more gently, they repaired the conversation when it started going sideways, and they kept a lot of warmth and humour in the room, even while disagreeing. In conflict discussions, stable couples had roughly five positive interactions for every negative one.

The therapy takes those findings and turns them into skills couples can learn.

The Sound Relationship House

The Gottman Method organizes a relationship into a model called the Sound Relationship House. You don't need to memorize it, but it's useful to know, because your therapist will likely use it to show you where you're strong and where you might want to build.

From the ground up, the levels are:

  1. Love maps. How well you know your partner's inner world: their worries, hopes, friends and history. This needs updating, because people change.
  2. Fondness and admiration. Noticing what your partner does right and saying it out loud, rather than mostly noticing what they get wrong.
  3. Turning toward. Responding to small bids for connection, like a comment, a question or a sigh. These everyday moments build trust over time.
  4. The positive perspective. When the first three levels are strong, partners give each other the benefit of the doubt. When they aren't, even neutral comments can feel like an attack.
  5. Managing conflict. Not ending conflict, but handling it in a way that doesn't damage the relationship.
  6. Making life dreams come true. Supporting each other's hopes and the things that give life meaning.
  7. Creating shared meaning. The rituals, values and stories you build together as a couple.

The two walls holding the house up are trust and commitment.

What happens in the first sessions

One thing that sets the Gottman Method apart is that it starts with a thorough assessment. It can feel slow when you're hurting and want to get to the "real work," but it means therapy is built around your relationship rather than a one-size-fits-all plan.

The assessment is usually spread over three sessions.

Session 1: together

You'll meet with the therapist as a couple, usually for 80 to 90 minutes. You'll go over consent and how therapy works, then talk about what brings you in. The therapist will also ask about the history of your relationship, like how you met and what drew you to each other. These stories say a lot about the friendship underneath the conflict.

Often the therapist will ask you to talk about an ongoing disagreement for a few minutes, so they can see how you communicate in real time. This can feel awkward, but you're not being graded. It's one of the most useful parts of the assessment.

You may also be asked to fill out questionnaires about your relationship between sessions.

Session 2: individual sessions

Each partner meets with the therapist alone, usually for about 45 minutes each. This is a chance to share your own history and your view of the relationship.

Many Gottman therapists use a no-secrets policy, which means anything shared individually may come up in joint sessions. Your therapist should explain this before your individual session, so you can decide what you want to share. These sessions are also where a therapist checks, privately, whether there are safety concerns.

Session 3: feedback and goals

You'll meet together again, usually for 80 to 90 minutes. The therapist will share what they noticed, including your strengths as a couple and areas to work on, often using the Sound Relationship House. Then you'll set goals for therapy together.

What sessions look like after that

After the assessment, most of the work happens in joint sessions. A few things you might notice:

  • You'll talk to each other more than to the therapist. In Gottman therapy, the therapist works a lot like a coach. Instead of each of you telling the therapist your side, the therapist helps you have a better conversation with each other, right there in the room.
  • Sessions are often longer. Gottman therapists often use 80 to 90 minute sessions, because it can take time for a conversation to get somewhere useful.
  • You'll practise skills, not just talk about them. That might mean redoing a conversation that went badly, or trying a new way to raise a concern.
  • There may be practice between sessions. Small exercises you try at home.
  • Therapy winds down gradually. As things improve, sessions usually space out, so you can test your skills on your own. Some therapists also offer check-ins after therapy ends.

What you'll actually work on

Every couple is different, but these are some of the patterns the Gottman Method focuses on most.

The Four Horsemen

Gottman's research identified four communication patterns that predict ongoing unhappiness when they show up often:

  • Criticism: attacking your partner's character instead of naming a specific issue ("You never think about anyone but yourself").
  • Contempt: eye-rolling, mockery and sarcasm that communicates disgust.
  • Defensiveness: counter-attacking or playing the victim instead of hearing the complaint.
  • Stonewalling: shutting down and withdrawing from the conversation.

Each one has an antidote. Criticism becomes a gentle start-up, where you talk about your feelings and what you need. Defensiveness becomes taking responsibility for even a small part of the problem. Contempt is countered by expressing your own feelings and needs and building a habit of appreciation. Stonewalling is countered by learning to calm your body before continuing.

Flooding

When an argument gets intense, your body can go into fight or flight. Gottman calls this flooding. Your heart rate climbs, and it becomes very hard to listen, no matter how much you want to.

In therapy, you'll learn to notice when you or your partner are flooded and how to take a real break: one that calms your body, rather than one spent rehearsing your next point. Gottman recommends breaks of at least 20 minutes.

Perpetual problems

In Gottman's research, about 69% of the time couples were arguing about perpetual problems: long-standing differences in personality or needs that may never fully go away. Think spender and saver, or one partner who wants to talk things out right away while the other needs time to think first.

The goal with these isn't to solve them. It's to move from feeling stuck to being able to talk about them with some understanding and even humour. I wrote more about this in why couples keep having the same argument.

Friendship and connection

A lot of couples come in focused on conflict, but the Gottman Method puts just as much weight on friendship. Couples who stay together tend to notice and respond to each other's small bids for connection. In one Gottman study, newlyweds who were still married six years later had turned toward each other's bids 86% of the time, compared with 33% for couples who divorced.

Is the Gottman Method right for us?

The Gottman Method can help with a wide range of concerns, including:

  • frequent arguments, or the same fight on repeat
  • feeling more like roommates than partners
  • communication that shuts down or escalates
  • big transitions, like moving in together, a new baby or a career change
  • wanting to strengthen a relationship that's already pretty good

It also works well for couples who like structure and practical tools.

When couples therapy may not be the right first step

If there's physical violence, threats, or you feel afraid of your partner, joint couples therapy may not be safe. This is one reason Gottman therapists meet with each partner individually during the assessment. In those situations, individual support and safety planning usually come first. If you're in BC and need support, you can call or text VictimLinkBC 24/7 at 1-800-563-0808. In an emergency, call 911.

Gottman vs EFT and other approaches

The Gottman Method isn't the only option. Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), developed by Canadian psychologist Dr. Sue Johnson, is another well-researched approach. EFT is grounded in attachment theory and focuses on the emotional cycle partners get caught in, and the needs for closeness and safety underneath it.

Broadly, the Gottman Method tends to be more skill-focused and structured, while EFT tends to focus more on emotions and attachment. Many couples therapists draw on more than one approach. What matters most is that you both feel safe and understood with the person you're working with.

What does "Gottman-trained" mean?

You'll see a few different terms on therapist profiles:

  • Gottman Level 1 or Level 2 trained: the therapist has completed one or more levels of the Gottman Institute's clinical training.
  • Certified Gottman Therapist (CGT): the therapist has completed all levels of training plus a certification process with consultation and video review. This takes years and is the highest designation.

I'm Gottman Level 1 trained, so I describe my couples work as Gottman-informed. I'm not a Certified Gottman Therapist. If certification matters to you, the Gottman Institute has a referral network you can search.

Frequently asked questions

How many sessions does Gottman couples therapy take?

It depends on your goals and what you're working on. The assessment usually takes about three sessions. After that, some couples come for a few months and others longer. Your therapist should check in with you regularly about progress.

Do we both need to come?

Gottman couples therapy is designed for both partners. If your partner isn't ready, individual counselling can still help you understand your own patterns and what you want from the relationship.

Will the therapist take sides?

No. A good couples therapist is on the side of the relationship. Part of the therapist's job is making sure both of you feel heard.

Is couples counselling covered by MSP in BC?

MSP doesn't cover private counselling in BC. Some extended health plans cover counselling, depending on the provider's credentials, so check your plan. My guide to free and low-cost counselling in BC lists more affordable options, including couples counselling.

What if we argue in a session?

That's okay, and it's actually useful. Seeing how you argue helps the therapist understand what's happening and coach you through it in the moment. If things get too heated, your therapist will help you slow down.

Can I see a student or intern counsellor for couples counselling?

Yes. Intern counsellors are supervised by experienced clinicians, and they're often more affordable. I wrote more about seeing a student counsellor in BC.

The bottom line

The Gottman Method is practical, structured and grounded in research, and it's designed so that you leave therapy with skills you can keep using. If you and your partner keep getting stuck in the same places, couples therapy can help you find a new way through. You don't have to wait until things are falling apart.
