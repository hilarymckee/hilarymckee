Counsellor vs psychotherapist vs psychologist in BC: what's the difference?

Hilary McKee Starting Therapy 8 min read Updated October 19, 2026

The short answer

In BC, a psychologist has a doctoral degree, is regulated by a provincial college and can assess and diagnose mental health conditions. A psychiatrist is a medical doctor who can diagnose and prescribe medication, and MSP covers their visits. "Counsellor" and "therapist" aren't protected titles, so training varies, but most qualified counsellors are Registered Clinical Counsellors (RCCs) with a master's degree. "Psychotherapist" becomes a protected title on November 29, 2027.

When you start looking for help, the titles can feel like alphabet soup. Counsellor, therapist, psychotherapist, psychologist, psychiatrist, RCC, RSW. Are they the same thing? Does it matter which one you see?

It does matter, though maybe not in the way you'd expect. Here's what each title actually means in BC, so you can choose with confidence.

Psychologists

A registered psychologist in BC holds a doctoral degree, either a PhD in psychology or a PsyD. Becoming registered also takes years of supervised experience and passing several exams, including an oral exam.

Psychologists are regulated by the College of Health and Care Professionals of BC (CHCPBC), which sets standards and handles complaints. They can assess, diagnose and treat mental health conditions, and some focus on formal psychological assessments. They don't prescribe medication.

If you see a psychologist in the public system, such as at a hospital or school, it's usually covered. Private psychologists aren't covered by MSP, though some extended health plans cover part of the cost.

A psychologist may be a good fit if you want a formal assessment or diagnosis, or you're dealing with complex mental health concerns.

Psychiatrists

A psychiatrist is a medical doctor who has specialized in mental health. Psychiatrists can diagnose mental illness and prescribe medication, and some also offer talk therapy.

Visits to a psychiatrist are covered by MSP, so you pay nothing with a valid BC Services Card. You usually need a referral from your family doctor or nurse practitioner.

A psychiatrist may be a good fit if you're wondering whether medication could help, or you need specialized care for a condition like bipolar disorder or psychosis.

Registered Clinical Counsellors (RCCs)

This is where most people in BC find a therapist. RCCs are members of the BC Association of Clinical Counsellors (BCACC). To become an RCC, a counsellor needs:

  • a master's degree in clinical counselling, psychotherapy or a closely related field
  • coursework in six core areas, including counselling theory, ethics and family therapy
  • at least 100 hours of clinical supervision
  • professional references

RCCs follow a code of ethics, and the BCACC has a process for complaints. But the BCACC is a professional association, not a government regulator, and membership is voluntary. RCCs don't diagnose or prescribe. Their focus is therapy itself: helping you understand what's happening, work through it and build new ways of coping and relating.

Many extended health plans cover RCCs, so check yours. For 2026, the BCACC recommends fees of about $140 to $175 for a 50-minute individual session, depending on experience, and higher for specialized services and couples. Some counsellors charge more or less.

You may also see CCC (Canadian Certified Counsellor), a national certification from the Canadian Counselling and Psychotherapy Association.

An RCC may be a good fit if you're dealing with anxiety, low mood, stress, relationship struggles, grief, life changes or past experiences you want to work through, or you simply want to understand yourself better.

Registered Therapeutic Counsellors (RTCs)

You may also see RTC, which stands for Registered Therapeutic Counsellor. It's a designation from the Association of Cooperative Counselling Therapists of Canada (ACCT), a professional association based in BC.

The main difference from an RCC is education. To become an RTC, a counsellor needs:

  • a counselling diploma, bachelor's degree or higher from a program of at least 880 hours
  • coursework that covers core areas, including counselling skills, ethics and models of therapy
  • at least 200 hours of direct client work and 50 hours of clinical supervision

An RCC, by comparison, needs a master's degree. Like the BCACC, the ACCT is a professional association with a code of ethics, not a government regulator.

That doesn't mean an RTC is the wrong choice. Many are skilled, caring counsellors, and some also hold a master's degree. But if you're using extended health benefits, check before you book. RTC is listed less often than RCC, psychologist, RSW or CCC among the designations plans commonly cover.

Registered Social Workers (RSWs)

Social workers in BC are regulated by the BC College of Social Workers under the Social Workers Act. Many work in hospitals, community agencies and government, and some offer counselling and therapy in private practice. Social workers often bring a strong focus on how families, communities and systems shape wellbeing.

"Counsellor" and "therapist"

Here's the part that surprises most people: in BC, anyone can call themselves a counsellor or a therapist. These titles aren't protected, so someone using them could have a master's degree and years of supervised training, or very little training at all.

That's why it's worth looking past the title and asking about designations, like RCC, CCC, psychologist or RSW. It's also why so many people in the field have pushed for regulation.

What about "psychotherapist"?

This is changing. On November 29, 2027, psychotherapy becomes a regulated profession in BC, overseen by the CHCPBC. From then on, only licensed practitioners will be able to call themselves psychotherapists. "Counsellor" and "counselling therapist" will stay unprotected.

The requirements to become a licensed psychotherapist are still being developed. I wrote more about what's changing in are counsellors regulated in BC?

Counselling vs psychotherapy: is there a difference?

People often use these words interchangeably, and many practitioners offer both. One common way to describe the difference: counselling tends to focus on building insight and skills to handle a challenge or transition, while psychotherapy tends to go deeper and longer, often with more significant mental health concerns. In practice, a good therapist adjusts to what you need.

How extended health benefits cover therapy

If you have extended health benefits through work, school or a partner's plan, they may cover a good part of the cost of therapy. Coverage usually depends on three things:

  • The practitioner's designation. Plans typically cover specific designations, such as RCC, registered psychologist, RSW or CCC. If your plan just says "counsellor" or "social worker," check which designations it accepts. Someone without a recognized designation usually won't be covered.
  • Your yearly maximum. Plans set a dollar limit per year, and it varies a lot, from a few hundred dollars to several thousand. Some plans also cap how much they'll pay per session.
  • Which category it falls under. Counselling is often listed under "paramedical" or "mental health practitioners" in your benefits booklet.

Coverage is growing. In 2026, the BCACC reported that 22 BC municipalities now cover RCCs in their employee benefit plans, up from 12 in 2023.

Before you book, ask your insurer or HR:

  • Which designations are covered for counselling?
  • What's my yearly maximum, and is there a per-session limit?
  • Do I need a doctor's referral?
  • Can my counsellor bill the plan directly, or do I submit receipts?
  • If my partner has a plan too, can we combine coverage?

It also helps to ask your counsellor for their designation and registration number, since insurers often want these on receipts.

So who should you see?

Here's a simple way to think about it:

  • If you want to talk things through and work on a challenge, an RCC, RSW or psychologist can all help. An RCC is often the most accessible and affordable option.
  • If you want a formal diagnosis or assessment, see a psychologist or talk to your family doctor.
  • If you're wondering about medication, start with your family doctor, who can refer you to a psychiatrist if needed.
  • If cost is a concern, check what your benefits cover, and see my guide to free and low-cost counselling in BC.

Whichever title you choose, what matters most is how you feel with the person. Research on therapy keeps coming back to the relationship between you and your therapist as one of the strongest predictors of whether therapy helps. If you want help thinking that through, my guide on how to choose the right therapist for you is a good next step.

If you're in crisis

If you're thinking about suicide or you're in crisis, call or text 9-8-8 any time. In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between a counsellor and a psychologist in BC?

A registered psychologist in BC has a doctoral degree, is regulated by the College of Health and Care Professionals of BC and can assess and diagnose mental health conditions. Most qualified counsellors are Registered Clinical Counsellors (RCCs) with a master's degree. Counsellors don't diagnose, and the title "counsellor" isn't protected.

What's the difference between a psychologist and a psychiatrist?

A psychiatrist is a medical doctor who can diagnose mental illness and prescribe medication, and visits are covered by MSP. A psychologist has a doctoral degree in psychology, can assess and diagnose, and provides therapy, but doesn't prescribe medication. Private psychologists aren't covered by MSP.

What does RCC mean?

RCC stands for Registered Clinical Counsellor, a designation from the BC Association of Clinical Counsellors. RCCs have a master's degree in counselling or a related field, completed coursework in core areas and at least 100 hours of clinical supervision.

What's the difference between an RCC and an RTC?

Both are counselling designations from BC professional associations, not government regulators. An RCC (Registered Clinical Counsellor) needs a master's degree and is granted by the BC Association of Clinical Counsellors. An RTC (Registered Therapeutic Counsellor) can qualify with a diploma, bachelor's degree or higher, plus supervised client hours, and is granted by the Association of Cooperative Counselling Therapists of Canada. Extended health plans cover RCCs more often, so check your plan.

Can a counsellor diagnose in BC?

No. Counsellors in BC can't diagnose mental health conditions. Psychologists and medical doctors, including psychiatrists, can.

Is "psychotherapist" a protected title in BC?

Not yet. From November 29, 2027, only practitioners licensed by the College of Health and Care Professionals of BC will be able to use the title "psychotherapist."

Will my extended health benefits cover counselling?

Often, yes. Most plans that cover counselling pay for practitioners with specific designations, such as RCCs, registered psychologists, RSWs or CCCs, up to a yearly maximum. Check your benefits booklet or ask your insurer which designations are covered, your yearly limit and whether you need a referral.

Does MSP cover counselling or psychologists?

MSP covers psychiatrists and mental health services in the public system. It doesn't cover private counselling or private psychologists, but many extended health plans cover some of the cost.
