Emotionally immature parents: signs, how it affects you, and how to heal.

Hilary McKee Relationships 10 min read Updated October 9, 2026

The short answer

An emotionally immature parent is one who struggles to notice, tolerate or respond to feelings, both their own and their child's. It isn't a diagnosis. It's a way of describing a pattern. Kids who grow up with it often become adults who are very tuned in to other people's moods and less sure of their own. The good news is that these patterns were learned, which means they can change. Naming the pattern, grieving what you didn't get, setting kinder expectations and working with a therapist can all help.

Some people can point to one big thing that went wrong in their childhood. Many can't. They had food on the table, a roof over their heads, maybe even parents who said "I love you." And still, something was missing. They felt alone with their feelings, or responsible for everyone else's.

If that sounds familiar, you might be one of the many people who grew up with an emotionally immature parent.

What is an emotionally immature parent?

The term was popularized by clinical psychologist Lindsay Gibson in her 2015 book Adult Children of Emotionally Immature Parents. She has since written several follow-ups, including Recovering from Emotionally Immature Parents (2019).

An emotionally immature parent may be a capable adult in many ways. They might hold down a job, run a household and be well liked by friends. Where they struggle is with emotional closeness. Feelings, especially big or uncomfortable ones, are hard for them to handle, so they tend to avoid them, dismiss them or make them about themselves.

Two things are worth saying up front:

  • It isn't a diagnosis. You won't find "emotionally immature parent" in the DSM. It's a helpful description, not a clinical label.
  • It usually isn't on purpose. Most emotionally immature parents were raised by people who couldn't meet their emotional needs either. Understanding that can make room for compassion. It doesn't erase the impact on you.

Signs of an emotionally immature parent

Every family is different, but these are patterns many people recognize:

  • Their mood set the tone for the whole house. You learned to read the room before you walked into it.
  • Your feelings were brushed off or turned around. "You're too sensitive," "stop crying" or a conversation that somehow ended up being about how they felt.
  • Conflict was avoided, or it exploded. There was little in between, and repair rarely happened.
  • You felt responsible for their feelings. You may have comforted a parent, kept the peace between your parents or tried hard not to be a burden.
  • They struggled to see you as your own person. Your interests, opinions or choices felt like a threat or an inconvenience rather than something to be curious about.
  • You felt lonely, even when they were there. This is one of the most common descriptions, and one of the hardest to explain.

Recognizing a few of these doesn't mean your parent was a bad person. It means some of your emotional needs may have gone unmet.

The four types of emotionally immature parents

In her book, Gibson describes four broad types. Most parents are a mix, but one style often stands out.

  1. The emotional parent. Moody and unpredictable, and often relies on others to help them feel stable.
  2. The driven parent. Busy, goal-focused and always trying to improve or perfect the people around them.
  3. The passive parent. Easygoing and often the "fun" parent, but avoids conflict and may not step in when their child needs protecting.
  4. The rejecting parent. Distant and dismissive, and seems to take little pleasure in spending time with their child.

How emotionally immature parents can affect you as an adult

Children need help understanding their feelings. When a parent can't offer that help, kids find their own ways to cope, and those ways often follow them into adulthood.

Research supports this. In a well-known 1996 study, psychologists John Gottman, Lynn Katz and Carole Hooven described the difference between parents who "coach" their children through emotions (noticing feelings, naming them, validating them and helping solve problems) and parents who "dismiss" emotions by ignoring, minimizing or trying to quickly shut them down. A 2025 review in Frontiers in Developmental Psychology found that parents who value and accept their children's emotions tend to have children with better emotion regulation and coping.

Some children also take on a parent's role at home, especially their emotional role: comforting a parent, acting as a confidant or managing the family's moods. Psychologists call this parentification. A 2023 systematic review of 95 studies found that parentification was commonly linked to anxiety, depression and other difficulties later on. It also found that some people developed real strengths, like strong coping skills, and that support from others made a difference.

Internalizers and externalizers

Gibson describes two common ways children cope with an emotionally immature parent:

  • Externalizers tend to act out their distress. They might be impulsive or look to others to fix their problems.
  • Internalizers turn it inward. They try to solve problems themselves, work harder and stay out of the way.

Her book focuses mostly on internalizers. If you're reading this, there's a good chance you're one. Some signs:

  • you notice everyone else's mood before your own
  • you apologize when someone else is upset, even when it has nothing to do with you
  • you're the one people call in a crisis, but you rarely ask for help yourself
  • you feel guilty saying no
  • you feel more comfortable giving care than receiving it
  • you're drawn to people who need you, and sometimes to people who aren't fully available

How it might show up in your adult life

The ways you coped as a child were smart. They helped you stay connected to the people you depended on. The trouble is that they can keep running long after you need them. Not everyone who grew up with an emotionally immature parent will relate to all of these, but many people recognize at least a few.

You're not always sure what you feel

If your feelings were ignored or brushed off growing up, you may have learned to set them aside. As an adult, that can look like saying "I'm fine" before you've checked, feeling numb or only realizing you were upset days later. It can also show up in your body first, as tension, headaches or exhaustion, before you have words for it.

You doubt yourself

When your reactions were often called "too much" or "overdramatic," it's easy to stop trusting them. You might second-guess your decisions, ask for lots of reassurance or assume that if there's a conflict, you must be the problem.

You're always scanning other people's moods

Reading the room kept you safe as a kid. As an adult, it can leave you on high alert. You might notice the smallest change in someone's tone, replay conversations afterwards or feel anxious when someone you care about seems upset, even when it has nothing to do with you.

Saying no feels impossible

If you were the one keeping the peace, other people's needs probably came first. Setting a boundary can bring up guilt, fear or the sense that you're being selfish. Many adult children say yes out of habit, then quietly feel resentful or burnt out.

You find yourself in familiar relationships

We tend to be drawn to what feels familiar, even when it isn't good for us. You might find yourself with partners or friends who are hard to reach emotionally, or working overtime to earn love that should feel freely given. Sometimes it goes the other way, and closeness itself feels uncomfortable, so you keep people at arm's length.

You feel lonely, even with people around

Emotional loneliness is one of the most common experiences adult children describe. You can have a full life, good friends and a loving partner and still feel like no one quite sees you. Often, that's because you've learned to share only the parts of yourself that feel safe.

You hold yourself to impossibly high standards

Some people learned that being helpful, high-achieving or low-maintenance was the surest way to get approval. As adults, that can turn into perfectionism, overworking or feeling like you have to earn rest.

Conflict feels like danger

If arguments at home were explosive, or were never talked about at all, you may not have seen what healthy conflict looks like. You might avoid disagreements, shut down when things get tense or feel like one argument could end the relationship. Repair, coming back together after a rupture, may feel unfamiliar.

Visits home leave you drained

Many adults notice they feel younger, smaller or more irritable around their parents. You might leave family visits exhausted, or slip back into old roles, like the peacekeeper or the responsible one, within minutes of walking in the door.

Becoming a parent brings it all up

Having your own kids can bring old feelings to the surface. You might feel overwhelmed by your child's big emotions, hear your parent's words come out of your mouth or feel grief as you give your child something you never received. This is a common time for people to seek support, and a powerful time to break the pattern.

If you recognized yourself in this list, please be gentle with yourself. None of these are character flaws. They're adaptations, and adaptations can be updated.

How to heal from emotionally immature parents

Healing doesn't mean your past disappears. It means it has less hold on how you live and love now. Here are some places to start.

1. Name the pattern

There is real relief in having words for something you've felt your whole life. Reading about emotional immaturity, or talking it through with someone you trust, can help you see that what happened was a pattern, not proof that something was wrong with you.

2. Make room for grief

Many adult children carry a quiet hope that their parent will one day become the parent they needed. Letting go of that hope is painful. It's also freeing. You can understand why your parent couldn't show up for you and still grieve that they didn't. Both can be true.

3. Adjust what you expect from them

Instead of hoping for a different response each time, try going into conversations with realistic expectations. If your parent has never been able to sit with your sadness, it may help to bring your sadness somewhere safer, like a friend, a partner or a therapist, and keep conversations with your parent lighter.

4. Practise small boundaries

Boundaries don't have to be dramatic. They can be as simple as ending a phone call when it turns critical, not sharing certain news or saying "I'm not going to talk about that today." Start small, and expect some discomfort. Guilt is common at first, especially for internalizers. It doesn't mean you're doing something wrong.

5. Offer yourself what you didn't get

Notice when you dismiss your own feelings the way they were once dismissed. Try responding to yourself the way a caring parent would: "That makes sense. Of course you're upset." Approaches like Internal Family Systems (IFS) can help you get to know the younger parts of you that learned to stay small or take care of everyone.

6. Work with a therapist

You don't have to untangle this alone. A therapist can help you understand your patterns and practise new ways of relating in a relationship where your feelings are welcome. Approaches that focus on attachment, emotions and parts work, like Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), attachment-based therapy and IFS, can be especially helpful here.

If you're not sure where to start, I wrote a guide to how to choose the right therapist for you.

Do I have to cut off my parents?

No. Some people choose to stay close, some keep a little more distance and some decide no contact is right for them. There isn't one correct answer, and it can change over time. What matters is that the relationship you have is one you choose with open eyes, rather than one you keep out of guilt or the hope that this time will be different.

Frequently asked questions

Is "emotionally immature parent" a diagnosis?

No. It's a term from Lindsay Gibson's work that describes a pattern of behaviour. It isn't listed in the DSM and isn't something a professional would diagnose.

How do emotionally immature parents affect adult children?

Common effects include trouble identifying your own feelings, self-doubt, people pleasing, guilt around boundaries, being drawn to emotionally unavailable partners, perfectionism and a lasting sense of emotional loneliness. These are learned patterns, and they can change with time and support.

Can emotionally immature parents change?

Some people do grow emotionally, especially if they choose to do their own work. But you can't make that happen for them. Healing works best when it focuses on what you can change: your expectations, your boundaries and how you care for yourself.

What's the best book on emotionally immature parents?

Lindsay Gibson's Adult Children of Emotionally Immature Parents is the most widely read starting point. Her follow-up, Recovering from Emotionally Immature Parents, is more focused on practical steps.

How do I know if I'm repeating the pattern with my own kids?

The fact that you're asking is a good sign. Emotional maturity isn't about never getting it wrong. It's about noticing, taking responsibility and repairing. Saying "I'm sorry I snapped. That wasn't about you" goes a long way.

The bottom line

If you grew up with an emotionally immature parent, the ways you learned to cope made sense. They helped you get through. As an adult, you get to keep what still serves you and gently let go of what doesn't. You deserve relationships where your feelings are welcome, including the one you have with yourself.
